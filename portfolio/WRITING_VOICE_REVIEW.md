# Review of James’s writing guidance

Reviewed: 2026-11-19. Companion to [WRITING_VOICE.md](./WRITING_VOICE.md).

## Assessment

The guide is useful and substantially supported by the sources. Its most distinctive insight is that James lets readers follow how an observation becomes a design question. Its main risk was making his voice uniformly casual, modest, and lightly edited. The essays show more range: direct advice, structured explanation, argument, and purposeful changes in pace. The guide and reusable prompt have been revised to accommodate that range.

This assessment is an interpretation of four writing samples, not a statistical analysis of James’s entire output. Differences between the pieces establish range; they do not by themselves establish how his voice evolved over time.

## Source and attribution checks

The guide links its verbatim article examples to the originating pieces. They are excerpts, not rewritten approximations. Analysis and suggested edits are labeled separately. The film dialogue and borrowed story template in the advice article are excluded as examples of James’s original sentences. Headings, framing, and commentary are relevant evidence of his writing choices.

The original Finestra narrative and James’s directly supplied reflection are stronger voice evidence than its recently edited opening, metadata, and captions. The current Forward Narrative Skill case is used below only as a factual brief, not as independent evidence of his natural prose. This distinction prevents assistant-authored copy from validating itself.

## Two paragraph comparisons

Both sides below are newly written illustrations. The “before” paragraphs demonstrate polished, abstract habits I can fall into when drafting case studies; they are not a claim that all AI writing sounds alike. The “after” paragraphs are attempts informed by the guide, not quotations from James or approved final website copy. Each pair uses the same source facts, with no added metrics, dialogue, or outcomes.

### 1. Finestra: an idea taking shape

**Before: generic AI-style draft**

My involvement in Atlanta Arts Movement and my experience of my father’s artwork revealed a recurring challenge: artists often had work in storage while potential buyers found purchasing art intimidating. In 2011, as mobile applications were becoming increasingly common, my brother Seth and I began exploring an app to connect local artists and buyers. We understood the opportunity as a communication problem and started developing initial concepts for a marketplace.

**After: an attempt in James’s voice**

I knew artists with artwork sitting in storage. My dad was one of them. He made incredible things in his spare time, but they rarely made it into someone else’s home. Meanwhile, people around me would say they weren’t creative or didn’t know what art to buy. In 2011, on the way to my brother Seth’s house, an app idea started to come together. We thought the gap between artists and buyers was a communication problem, and a phone seemed like an interesting place to start.

**What changed:** The second paragraph stays with people and things the reader can picture. It lets the idea arrive during the story and preserves the provisional nature of the early thinking. The first is accurate but reads like a summary written after the interesting parts were removed. The shift goes beyond adding contractions or shortening sentences. [Facts and voice reference: Finestra, Process and Ideas](../content/projects/finestra-art-app/index.md).

### 2. Forward Narrative Skill: explaining a workflow problem

**Before: generic AI-style draft**

The original narrative workflow created a dependency on a single author and a multi-day publication process. I addressed this bottleneck by designing and maintaining a Claude Skill that enables teammates to create and share narratives through Dispatch without a code deployment. Our CTO leads Dispatch, and we collaborated on the MCP interface and the division of responsibilities. Teammates now use the skill weekly for sales demonstrations and design prototypes.

**After: an attempt in James’s voice**

At first, every narrative had to go through me. I’d make it locally, open a pull request, and wait for review, merge, and deployment before anyone could share a link. That could take days. I built a Claude Skill so teammates could create and share narratives through Dispatch without that deployment process. Our CTO leads Dispatch; we worked together on its MCP interface and where the skill’s responsibilities should end. Teammates now use it each week for demos and design prototypes.

**What changed:** The second paragraph describes the actual sequence instead of naming a bottleneck and announcing that it was addressed. A short sentence gives the delay weight. The technical terms and collaborator credit remain because they explain how the work happened. It does not manufacture a joke or personal anecdote for a technical paragraph. [Factual brief: Forward Narrative Skill](../content/projects/forward-narrative-skill/index.md); [voice reference: Finestra](../content/projects/finestra-art-app/index.md).

## Practical acceptance criteria

The updated prompt now asks an editor to preserve concrete context, reasoning, ownership, uncertainty, and useful personality while choosing a register for the destination. It explicitly permits technical terms, structure, deliberate repetition, and clear outcome claims when supported.

Review each draft for three things: whether its facts and meaning survived; whether a reader can follow the actual thought or experience; and whether the prose has become overly uniform or generic. Do not require every paragraph to have a joke, a question, a metaphor, or a short emphatic sentence. James’s review remains the strongest check on whether a proposed revision sounds like him.
