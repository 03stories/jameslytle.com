## Story skeleton

### Context: what was happening?

James had spent several years organizing a local arts community and saw working artists—including his father—accumulate finished work without practical ways to reach buyers. In 2011, the App Store created a new channel for tackling small communication and discovery problems.

### Real problem: what tension or consequence mattered?

Artists wanted their work appreciated but often lacked time and administrative skills for promotion. Potential buyers enjoyed art but felt intimidated by discovering, evaluating, and imagining original work in their own homes. Solving only one side would not create a viable marketplace.

### Role and boundaries

- Scope: Co-founder, complete app UX/UI ownership, product direction, research, marketing, and end-to-end marketing-site design and engineering.
- Team: James and his brother/co-founder, with artists and potential users contributing feedback.
- Responsibilities: Complete app UX/UI design, marketing-site design and engineering, most Preview web development, promotional video, customer/problem framing, and broad business participation.
- Personally owned: The entire app design and the marketing site's design and engineering.
- Collaborator ownership: His co-founder owned native iOS engineering, including implementation of the wall-scale innovation.

### Decision 1: Focus on local original art

- Situation: Existing digital competitors focused on high-end professional art or mass-produced prints.
- Options: Enter an established art-commerce category or build around original local work and underrepresented everyday artists.
- Constraint: The team needed to serve both time-constrained artists and buyers who did not identify as art collectors.
- Decision: Build a local marketplace centered on real original pieces from nearby artists.
- Result: The product required linked artist capture/promotion and buyer discovery experiences.
- Evidence: Competitor survey, IA sketch, search/filter/map wireframes, and artist capture flows.

### Decision 2: Help buyers connect with unfamiliar art

- Situation: Buyers struggled to interpret artwork and imagine it in their own space.
- Options: Follow the conventional dimensions-only listing pattern or create an experience that showed a piece at scale on the buyer's own wall.
- Constraint: Device sensors and native spatial tooling were limited at the time, increasing technical risk and reducing precision.
- Decision: Prioritize wall-scale preview because spatial context could create a stronger bridge between artists and serious potential buyers; artist-recorded audio added another personal connection.
- Result: Wall-scale preview became Finestra's most talked-about capability and later justified a focused spinout experience.
- Evidence: Artwork, capture, audio-recording, and Preview assets; linked product videos.

### Decision 3: Reduce the product to its strongest feature

- Situation: After launch, wall-scale preview remained the clearest source of delight.
- Options: Continue emphasizing the full marketplace or isolate that value into a simpler, more shareable product.
- Constraint: Marketplace complexity made the core interaction harder to distribute independently.
- Decision: Build Preview around art capture and a shareable web link for wall-scale viewing.
- Result: The team shipped a simpler app/web experience; adoption evidence is unknown.
- Evidence: Preview overview image, linked video, and James's web-frontend ownership statement.

### What shipped?

The Finestra marketplace app, its marketing site and promotional video, and the later Preview app/web experience. The repository contains wireframes, product screens, an archived marketing-site capture, and Preview material.

### Outcome and reflection

- Quantitative: More than 60 artists and 200 artworks participated in the marketplace.
- Qualitative: The team received interest from multiple U.S. cities, alumni-magazine coverage, conference exposure, and recurring delight around wall-scale preview.
- Business/organizational: The startup operated for several years, showed adoption and expansion interest, and then closed.
- Learning: A two-sided marketplace required more early buyer research and better facilitation of the exchange, not just an appealing product concept.
- What I would change now: Invest considerably more in buyer interviews and exchange mechanics before expanding features.
