## How to interpret the guidance

Distinguish three kinds of guidance:

- **Observed patterns:** examples of how James writes in this small set of sources. They are strong references, not requirements for every paragraph.
- **Current preferences:** James’s explicit requests, including fewer em dashes and less repetition of “product” in hero statements. These take precedence over habits found in older work.
- **Editorial recommendations:** improve grammar, check claims, trim unhelpful tangents, and preserve ownership. These are editing judgments, not proof that the original prose always follows those rules.

Prioritize factual accuracy and James’s current instructions, then the piece’s audience and purpose, then stylistic resemblance. Sample size and differences in genre prevent claiming that every observed habit is a permanent personal trait.

## What the writing sounds like

James sounds like a thoughtful designer walking someone through work he cared about. The writing is personal, curious, explanatory, and candid. He is comfortable being excited about an idea and admitting where his understanding was incomplete. His confidence comes through the decisions and work he describes, with room for uncertainty and hindsight. In essays, it also comes through explicit arguments and recommendations; do not remove a supported point of view in pursuit of modesty.

There is warmth in the attention he gives people: his father, his brother, local artists, prospective buyers, and the people whose responsibilities he understood better after running a business. Technology matters because of what it lets those people experience.

## What the articles add

The Finestra-only description understated the voice’s range. James can also be playful, pointed, and openly opinionated. The recurring move is to connect an ordinary observation to a larger design or human question. Warmth does not require a soft claim, and clarity does not require removing personality.

### Opinion: conviction with room for possibility

Verbatim examples from [Another Hot Data Trend](https://medium.com/@03STORIES/another-hot-data-trend-same-timeless-goal-98b631964c49):

> But it won’t. At least not yet.

> This is ok. This is normal.

> It’s good work.

The short sentences interrupt a longer argument and change its pace. James challenges inflated expectations, then leaves room for useful progress. The ending encourages continued effort rather than finishing with a sweeping innovation claim. His outlook combines skepticism about easy answers with hope for what people can accomplish.

**Editing implication:** Preserve a clear position and occasional blunt sentence. Do not hedge every opinion or turn every paragraph into a punchline. Historical predictions need checking before reuse.

### Teaching: playful observation makes the concept approachable

Verbatim examples from [Visualization techniques](https://medium.com/@03STORIES/visualization-techniques-we-all-knew-at-4-years-old-b2953edfa2aa):

> Thankfully, she also acknowledged my hair.

> They’re just principles after all; not laws.

> Let’s see what each principle would say…

A family drawing becomes a way to explain visual grouping. The humor is affectionate and lightly directed at himself. He gives abstract principles a speaking role, then returns to the drawing to make them tangible. This is more expressive than merely using plain language.

**Editing implication:** Keep the concrete example doing the explanatory work. Retain a useful aside; do not add an unrelated joke to simulate warmth. Give readers a way to see the idea before asking them to accept the abstraction.

### Advice: involve the reader and make the next step useful

Verbatim examples from [Data Discussion Etiquette](https://medium.com/@03STORIES/data-discussion-etiquette-from-brad-pitt-9d97ae977ec6):

> Let’s break down the key moments.

> Why is this useful for me to know?

> Be enjoyably useful.

> Don’t leave people hanging.

James invites the reader into an exercise, voices an audience question, and uses direct instructions as headings. The film supplies an accessible frame for practical advice. His own headings and commentary are the voice evidence; the character’s dialogue is borrowed material.

**Editing implication:** Use a direct question or invitation when it helps readers follow or act. Finish with a relevant next step when the piece calls for one. Do not bolt a sales CTA onto every reflection.

## Choose the register for the piece

| Context | Emphasize | Avoid forcing |
|---|---|---|
| Project case study | Lived context, decisions, contribution, artifacts, hindsight | An industry argument or a full personal backstory on every page |
| Explanatory article | A tangible example, shared observation, playful explanation, practical application | A dry list of rules or compulsory jokes |
| Opinion essay | A position, supporting reasoning, deliberate emphasis, constructive possibility | Constant qualification or unsupported certainty |
| Hero or short introduction | A clear thought and natural cadence | Compressing all of the voice’s traits into one line |

His older use of em dashes does not override his current preference for fewer of them. Preserve deliberate repetition when it builds an argument; cut repetition that only restates the same explanation. Do not automatically replace colorful verbs or familiar imagery with neutral business language.

Structure is useful: the teaching and advice articles use principles and headings. Choose an appropriate structure instead of banning templates categorically. Contrast and repeated sentence openings can clarify an argument; avoid them when they become an automatic way to make every observation sound profound. These are observations of the advice article’s organization and the AI essay’s rhetorical sequencing, respectively. [Advice source](https://medium.com/@03STORIES/data-discussion-etiquette-from-brad-pitt-9d97ae977ec6); [essay source](https://medium.com/@03STORIES/another-hot-data-trend-same-timeless-goal-98b631964c49).

Plain language can still carry an ambitious idea or a precise technical term. Define or ground an unfamiliar term when needed; do not substitute a vaguer phrase just to make the writing casual.

## Patterns to preserve

| Pattern | Evidence from Finestra | How to use it |
|---|---|---|
| Begin with something noticed in life | “We have lots of artwork sitting in our closets” and the story of his father’s artwork | When the source supports it, introduce the concrete situation that made the problem matter. Let the larger design question emerge from it. |
| Show how understanding changed | “This didn't really seem like a problem to me, because it was normal.” | Preserve the movement from an assumption to a question or realization. Readers should understand how James arrived at a decision. |
| Explain what people might feel or do | “Even if people didn't quite connect with the visual language yet, we thought they might connect with a personal audio clip…” | Connect a feature to the human possibility behind it. Preserve the distinction between an expectation and an observed result. |
| Let enthusiasm show plainly | “Apps were the hot new thing in our world.” | Keep a little of the excitement of being there. Simple, situated enthusiasm works better than promotional adjectives. |
| Use casual, reader-facing transitions | “Ok, back to business.” and “Next, let's jump ahead to post-launch.” | A brief conversational signpost can help readers move through a long story. Use naturally and sparingly; these exact phrases are not required. |
| Give artifacts a direct introduction | “Here is one of the first, most basic wireframes” | Tell the reader what they are about to see and why it belongs in the story. Avoid elaborate descriptions of ordinary process material. |
| Reflect with specific ownership | “I think we didn't pay enough early attention…” | Name the missed opportunity and what James would change. Keep appropriate “I” and “we” distinctions. |
| Use familiar imagery to explain ideas | “Creativity is a muscle” and an idea that “bubbled to the surface” | An everyday metaphor can make the thought approachable. Keep useful ones from the source; do not add a metaphor to every paragraph. |
| Allow mixed feelings | The supplied reflection pairs “a bit cringy” with pride in the experiences and achievements | Preserve affection, discomfort, pride, and learning when James expresses them. An older project can matter without every part needing praise or a formal disclaimer. |

## Vocabulary, rhythm, and structure

The natural vocabulary is mostly everyday language mixed with design terms when useful: artists, buyers, ideas, artwork, feedback, wireframes, prototypes, UX flows. James often uses verbs such as “noticed,” “knew,” “wondered,” “wanted,” “thought,” and “decided.” These make his reasoning visible. Keep them where they convey an actual perspective rather than stripping all qualification out of the copy.

The rhythm alternates between longer explanations and short, conversational observations. Contractions, occasional asides, and small moments of humor belong. Technical specificity is welcome when it explains what he did; abstract terminology should not replace a concrete account.

The Finestra story generally moves from personal context to a problem, an idea, experiments, artifacts, shipped work, and reflection. That is a useful option for a full case study. A short project introduction may need only the problem, contribution, and result. Preserve the reasoning without making every page retell the whole chronology.

“I” tends to carry personal observation, contribution, or hindsight. “We” carries shared intent and team decisions. Preserve those boundaries rather than making everything sound independently authored or anonymously collective.

## What to improve without losing the voice

- Break up sentences that contain several different thoughts. Keep some variation in sentence length so the result still sounds conversational.
- Trim repeated explanations, especially when a tangent delays the project story. Keep the personal detail that explains why the work mattered.
- Correct typos, missing words, punctuation, and usage. The original’s rough edges are editing opportunities, not stylistic traits to imitate.
- Keep uncertainty where it is meaningful. “We thought” is appropriate for a hypothesis; an observed outcome should say what actually happened.
- Replace broad claims with supported specifics, or flag them for verification. A confident historical recollection does not establish market-wide novelty.
- Make ownership clearer without inflating it. Credit collaborators and distinguish design, implementation, and business responsibilities.
- Use periods, commas, or a new sentence in place of most em dashes. Keep the titles of published works intact.
- Retain candid words such as “cringy” when James supplies them. Do not spread self-deprecation across unrelated work or turn it into an apology.

## Small editing examples

These are illustrative edits, not changes to the published project.

**Original:** “This didn't really seem like a problem to me, because it was normal.”

**Light edit:** “This didn’t really seem like a problem to me because it was normal.”

The observation is already clear and personal. It needs very little intervention.

**Original:** “Eventually we decided we wanted to try making that 1 feature as simple and as shareable as possible.”

**Light edit:** “Eventually, we wanted to see how simple and shareable we could make that one feature.”

This removes extra words while keeping the sense of an experiment. “We optimized our core differentiator for distribution” would lose both the plain language and the exploratory intent.

## Reusable prompt

Copy the block below with the draft or source notes:

```text
Help me write or improve this piece in my voice, James Lytle’s. Use the supplied draft and notes as the factual source.

I sound like a thoughtful designer walking someone through work I cared about: personal, curious, practical, warm, and candid. I explain what I noticed, why it mattered to people, what we tried, and how my understanding changed. I can be excited about an idea and honest about what I missed. I can also be playful and pointed: give a clear opinion when the material supports it, and leave room for useful possibilities. Do not make every claim cautious or every sentence neutral.

For an existing draft, make the lightest edits that improve clarity and flow. Preserve distinctive observations, useful personal details, and language that already sounds like me. For new writing, build from the supplied details; do not invent anecdotes, feelings, dialogue, decisions, or results to create a personal tone.

Use everyday language, contractions, and a natural mix of short observations and longer explanations. Use design and technical terms where they help explain the actual work. Let readers follow my reasoning. Connect features to what people could experience or do. A little humor or a casual transition is welcome when it fits, but don’t manufacture quirks or repeatedly copy signature phrases.

Choose a register that fits: reflective and specific for a case study, playful and explanatory for teaching, more direct and opinionated for an essay. Ground an abstract idea in a concrete example when one is supplied. Preserve occasional short sentences for emphasis, reader-facing questions, and deliberate repetition when they serve the thought. Do not turn these into a repeated formula or add an anecdote to every piece.

Use “I” for my contribution and perspective, and “we” for shared work. Keep collaborator credit and ownership accurate. Distinguish what we hoped would happen from what we observed. Keep meaningful uncertainty and specific hindsight. Let concrete work and evidence support claims about leadership, innovation, or impact. Keep useful outcomes and a clear point of view; remove inflated conclusions that the evidence cannot support.

Clean up grammar, repetition, and tangents without flattening the personality. Use em dashes sparingly. Avoid generic portfolio language, abstract lessons that could apply to any project, and rigid problem/process/solution formatting when a more natural story works. Keep the length and structure appropriate to the destination. Use headings, lists, contrast, and technical terms when they make the thought clearer. Keep deliberate emphasis and repetition; remove accidental redundancy. Do not introduce slang, typos, jokes, or clipped sentences just to sound human.

If a claim needs evidence or a missing detail would materially change the story, ask a focused question rather than filling it in. Ask about missing personal experiences, intentions, or feelings when they would help the piece; do not invent them for tone alignment. Continue with independently supported sections while waiting, and keep any dependent passage unresolved. Treat supplied quotations marked as exact as verbatim; otherwise correct ordinary copy errors.

Before returning the draft, check that no new facts, intentions, or feelings have slipped in. Check that the reasoning, collaborator credit, and degree of certainty remain intact. Read for natural cadence without requiring every paragraph to display a stylistic signature. Return the revised draft. Briefly flag any factual questions or substantial changes to meaning separately.

Destination and audience: [fill in]
Desired length and editing scope: [fill in]
Draft or source notes: [paste here]
```

## Review questions

Before accepting a revision, check: Does it still sound like someone who was there? Can the reader follow what James noticed and why he made the choices he did? Are the human stakes visible? Did the edit preserve the most personal, useful details? Are contributions and outcomes accurate? Did clarity improve without making the piece sound like a generic case study? Did the edit preserve useful humor, a clear point of view, and purposeful changes in pace? Does the register fit the destination?
